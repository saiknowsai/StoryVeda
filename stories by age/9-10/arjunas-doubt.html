🏹 Mahabharata

Arjuna's Doubt

On the battlefield of Kurukshetra, Arjuna's heart trembled. But Krishna's words gave him the greatest courage of all.

Ages 7–10 📖 10 min read ✦ Dharma ✦ Courage
यदा यदा हि धर्मस्य ग्लानिर्भवति भारत "Whenever dharma declines, O Arjuna, I arise." — Bhagavad Gita 4.7
Scene one

Two Great Armies, Face to Face

The sun had barely risen over the vast plain of Kurukshetra. The air was thick with the sound of conch shells, war drums and the restless stamping of horses. Two enormous armies stood facing each other — thousands of warriors, elephants and chariots, stretching as far as the eye could see.

On one side stood the Pandavas — five brothers who had been cheated of their kingdom and had fought for years to reclaim it. On the other stood the Kauravas — their cousins, who had taken what was not theirs by right.

Today was the day it would all be decided.

Scene two

A Trembling Heart

Arjuna was the greatest archer in the world. No one could match his skill. But as he looked across the battlefield, something changed inside him.

He could see familiar faces in the enemy army. His teachers, his uncles, his cousins — people he had grown up with, eaten meals with, laughed with as a child. His hands began to shake. His famous bow Gandiva slipped from his fingers. His knees felt weak.

"Krishna," he whispered to his dear friend and charioteer, "I cannot do this. How can I fight my own family? What is the point of a kingdom if everyone I love is gone? I would rather give up everything and become a beggar."

And with that, the great Arjuna sat down in his chariot and wept.

Scene three

Krishna's Gentle Smile

Krishna looked at his dear friend with great love. He did not scold Arjuna. He did not laugh at him. He understood — because he understood everything.

Gently, he placed a hand on Arjuna's shoulder.

"Dear Arjuna," Krishna said softly, "you are grieving for those who do not need your grief. A wise person does not mourn for the living or the dead."

And then, slowly, Krishna began to speak — and his words were like a lamp lit in a dark room.

Scene four

The Wisdom of the Soul

Krishna explained to Arjuna something wonderful — that the true self, the soul inside each person, can never be hurt or destroyed. It is like a flame that no wind can blow out. The body changes, grows old and one day stops — but the soul lives on, always.

"So do not grieve," Krishna said, "for those you see before you. You are not fighting their souls. You are simply fulfilling your duty — your dharma — as a warrior and a protector of what is right."

He told Arjuna that doing one's duty — without worrying about reward or loss, without fear — is the greatest form of courage there is.

Scene five

Courage Born From Within

As Krishna spoke, something changed in Arjuna. The trembling stopped. The confusion cleared. It was as if a great fog had lifted and the sun was shining through.

Arjuna realised that his sadness had come from thinking only of himself — his feelings, his fears, his grief. But real courage meant thinking of what was right, not just what was comfortable.

He picked up his bow Gandiva. His hands were steady now. His eyes were clear.

"Krishna," he said, "my doubt is gone. I understand now. I am ready."

✦ What we learn from this story

Courage is doing what is right, even when it is hard

Arjuna was not weak because he felt afraid. Even the greatest warriors feel fear and doubt. What made him brave was listening, thinking clearly and choosing to do what was right — even when it was the hardest thing in the world. True courage always starts on the inside.

🪔 Talk about it together

  • Have you ever had to do something difficult even though you were scared? How did it feel?
  • Why do you think Arjuna felt sad when he saw people he loved on the other side?
  • Krishna said the soul can never be hurt. What do you think that means?
  • What does doing your duty mean to you — at school or at home?

The Bhagavad Gita is one of the most beloved texts in the world. It was spoken by Krishna to Arjuna on the battlefield of Kurukshetra and contains some of the deepest wisdom ever put into words — about duty, courage, the soul and the meaning of a good life.
